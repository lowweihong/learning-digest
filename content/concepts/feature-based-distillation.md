## Definition
A distillation technique where the student network is trained to replicate internal intermediate feature maps, hidden layer activations, or attention distributions generated by intermediate layers of the teacher, rather than matching only final output logits.

## Why it's true
Relying solely on final output logits forces a student to discover all intermediate feature hierarchies on its own. For deep architectures or complex downstream tasks (such as object detection and segmentation), output logits provide too weak or sparse a supervision signal to guide early and middle layers.

Feature-based distillation (pioneered by FitNets) breaks the learning process into intermediate checkpoints:
1. **Hint and Guided Layers:** A chosen middle layer in the teacher acts as a "hint," and a corresponding layer in the student is designated as the "guided layer."
2. **Dimension Alignment ($1 \times 1$ Convolutions):** Because the student network typically has narrower channels than the teacher (e.g., $C_S = 64$ vs. $C_T = 256$), a trainable projection (such as a $1 \times 1$ convolution or linear matrix) projects the student's feature maps into the teacher's channel dimension.
3. **Loss Minimization:** The Euclidean distance (L2 loss) or cosine similarity between the aligned feature maps is minimized:
   $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{feature}} = \frac{1}{2} \| F_T - W_{\text{proj}} * F_S \|_2^2$$
4. **Attention Transfer:** Instead of matching raw feature tensors element-by-element, students can match spatial attention maps (summing absolute activation values along the channel axis, $A = \sum_c |F_c|^2$) or token-to-token attention matrices in Transformers.

## Minimal example
Suppose a ResNet-50 teacher produces a stage-3 feature map $F_T \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 14 \times 14}$, while a MobileNet student produces $F_S \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 14 \times 14}$:
- To align them, apply a $1 \times 1$ convolution $W_{\text{proj}}$ with $64$ input channels and $256$ output channels:
  $$\hat{F}_S = W_{\text{proj}} * F_S \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times 14 \times 14}$$
- The mean squared error loss drives the student's intermediate filters to capture the same spatial structures, edges, and object parts that the teacher's larger capacity discovered.

## Common confusions
- **Forcing student channels to equal teacher channels:** You do not need to construct the student with the same channel width as the teacher. A lightweight, trainable $1 \times 1$ convolutional adapter resolves dimensional mismatches during training and is discarded at inference.
- **Over-constraining the student:** Forcing a small student to match every single intermediate activation across all layers can severely constrain its limited capacity, leading to worse performance. Aligning only a handful of strategic intermediate stages works best.
